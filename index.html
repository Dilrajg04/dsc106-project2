Proposition: Officers with substantiated misconduct still get promoted.

Dataset: Civilian Complaints Against New York City Police Officers.

FOR the Proposition

The NYPD fails to hold officers accountable, where officers with more upheld misconduct complaints are more likely to be promoted, leading to misconduct being effectively rewarded instead of punished.

Design Decisions and Rationale:

  • Y-axis truncated at 40% (Score -2.0): The y-axis is started at 40% instead of 0%, which makes the 5+ bar look 4.5x as high as the 1 bar when it is only 1.5x as high. The y-axis stops at 80%, which makes each bar taller than they should. In combination, they artificially inflated the perceived trend to make it steeper than the actual trend in data, making the finding more shocking and memorable for the audience.
  • Red bar with brightness that scales (Score -1.5): The bars are colored red to emphasize the result is undesired, and the bar is made darker the higher the percentage promoted, adding visual weight to make the reader uncomfortable. The darker color also draws the reader's attention to the right side of the plot, highlighting the trend and making them less likely to notice the truncated axis.
  • Value labels and bolded labels (Score -1.0): Value labels are added on top of each bar so the audience never has to look at the y-axis, which makes them less likely to notice the truncated axis. The 60% and 72% are bolded to add visual weight and further draw attention to the right.
  • Annotation on the rightmost bar (Score +1.0): The annotation says nearly 3 in 4 officers with 5+ complaints were promoted. Where the bar implies trend visually, the annotation expresses the trend in word to help readers understand the meaning and implication of the number. It is not deceptive and simply helps express and convey the trend more clearly.
  • Lack of control variable (Score -2.0): It is possible that the trend is caused by a confound, where officers who get more complaints simply serve longer and are thus more likely to be promoted. This is completely omitted in the chart to avoid weakening the message, which is deceptive. In reality, even with the service time controlled, the trend is still significant but is weaker, indicating a real trend that cannot be fully explained by service time.

AGAINST the Proposition

The NYPD takes accountability with a working disciplinary system, where a substantiated misconduct finding hurts the officer's career, and most officers with one are never promoted beyond their initial rank.

Design Decisions and Rationale:

  • Relative Framing, “30% lower,” (Score -1.5): The absolute gap is 8 percentage points (27% vs 19%), but 30% lower sounds much bigger. The word “odds” is also deceiving, we computed relative risk, while the actual odds ratio is about 0.63 or 37% lower odds. Writing “8 points lower” is more accurate but less effective.
  • Comparing against exonerated and unsubstantiated groups, dashed average line (score +1): Using cleared officers as the comparison group is proper control, and the two groups are nearly identical (28% vs 27%), the dashed line makes the gap easy to visualize vs a plain graph alternative.
  • 0 based axis with contrast highlighted bars (Score +1.5): Starting the axis at 0 keeps the bar lengths proportional to true values. The blue highlight directs attention to the key group without distorting it. The highlight makes the graph more alive compared to a flat all gray graph initially.

Final Reflection

Throughout this project, we learned how much the way data is presented can influence people's opinions. One of the easiest parts was probably choosing which information to highlight to support each side of our proposition. It was more difficult to create two different arguments that are convincing using the same dataset. For example, when supporting our proposition, we focused more on the fact that 72% of officers with five or more upheld misconduct complaints were promoted. While on the other side, we highlighted that 78% of officers were never promoted after their first substantiated complaint. Both of these statistics came from the same dataset, but audiences can come up with different conclusions depending on how the data is presented.

What surprised us most was how small design choices could affect how people understand the data. For example, when we changed the y-axis to start at 40% instead of 0%, the difference in promotion rates looked much bigger than it actually was. We also noticed that using certain words in the titles could make the results sound more dramatic. This made us realize that even if the numbers are accurate, the way we present them can still mislead readers. After seeing this, we realized that ethical visualization is not just about having accurate numbers, but also about making sure the way we present the data doesn't give people the wrong idea.

We think that persuasive design choices are acceptable as long as they help people actually understand the data without hiding important information. For example, highlighting or bolding certain words with different colors is fine as it helps the reader focus on what is important in the design. However, it can become misleading if the design makes differences seem bigger than they really are or leave out any detail that can change how the audience understands the result. We also agree that it can be hard to know the difference between acceptable and misleading choices because it depends on how the data is presented. Overall, this project taught us to be more careful when creating graphs and to think about how our design choices might affect the audience.
