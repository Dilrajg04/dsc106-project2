FOR the Proposition

The NYPD fails to hold officers accountable, where officers with more upheld misconduct complaints are more likely to be promoted, leading to misconduct being effectively rewarded instead of punished.

Design Decisions and Rationale:

  • Binning upheld misconduct into 1 / 2 / 3–4 / 5+ (Score: −0.5) [PLACE HOLDER]
  • Defining "promoted" as any higher rank, including detective (Score: −1) [PLACE HOLDER]
  • Starting the y-axis at 40% (Score: −2) [PLACE HOLDER]
  • A red gradient that darkens as misconduct increases (Score: −0.5) [PLACE HOLDER]
  • Causal title and the "Nearly 3 in 4" callout (Score: −1) [PLACE HOLDER]

AGAINST the Proposition

The NYPD takes accountability with a working disciplinary system, where a substantiated misconduct finding hurts the officer's career, and most officers with one are never promoted beyond their initial rank.

Design Decisions and Rationale:

  • Patrol officers only, with promotion to Sergeant or above (Score: +1.5) We limited the data to only patrol officers because they start at the same rank. We also only counted promotions to Sergeant and above, because a detective assignment is a designation rather than a civil-service promotion. Including other ranks would have inflated the numbers.
  • Comparing three outcome groups (Score: +1) We pooled from three outcome groups (exonerated, unsubstantiated, and substantiated) so the audience could see the difference between officers who were cleared against those who were not. Cleared officers have nearly identical promotion rates.
  • Title: "cuts the odds of making sergeant by 30%" (Score: −1.5) [PLACE HOLDER]
  • Dashed average line and "30% lower" arrow (Score: −1) We used a dashed line to show the average of the two comparison groups to clarify the 30% difference in the title. The "30% lower" blue line with an arrow shows the relative difference between the group average which required the axis to start at 0%.
  • Blue highlight, gray comparison bars, axis from zero (Score: +0.5) We used the blue color to highlight our key group while using gray to differentiate it from the comparison groups.

Final Reflection

Throughout this project, we learned how much the way data is presented can influence people's opinions. One of the easiest parts was probably choosing which information to highlight to support each side of our proposition. It was more difficult to create two different arguments that are convincing using the same dataset. For example, when supporting our proposition, we focused more on the fact that 72% of officers with five or more upheld misconduct complaints were promoted. While on the other side, we highlighted that 78% of officers were never promoted after their first substantial complaint. Both of these statistics came from the same dataset, but audiences can come up with different conclusions depending on how the data is presented.

What surprised us most was how small design choices could affect how people understand the data. For example, when we changed the y-axis to start at 40% instead of 0%, the difference in promotion rates looked much bigger than it actually was. We also noticed that using certain words in the titles could make the results sound more dramatic. This made us realize that even if the numbers are accurate, the way we present them can still mislead readers. After seeing this, we realized that ethical visualization is not just about having accurate numbers, but also about making sure the way we present the data doesn't give people the wrong idea.

We think that persuasive design choices are acceptable as long as they help people actually understand the data without hiding important information. For example, highlighting or bolding certain words with different colors is fine as it helps the reader focus on what is important in the design. However, it can become misleading if the design makes differences seem bigger than they really are or leave out any detail that can change how the audience understands the result. We also agree that it can be hard to know the difference between acceptable and misleading choices because it depends on how the data is presented. Overall, this project taught us to be more careful when creating graphs and to think about how our design choices might affect the audience.
